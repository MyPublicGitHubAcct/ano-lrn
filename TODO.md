### Add wave digital filter diode clipper

Add `notebooks/virtual_analog_wdf.ipynb` demonstrating the diode clipper alongside `soft_clip` and `wavefold`. WDF simulation is the only method in this codebase that derives clipping character directly from physical component values, making it the most accurate path to VCV Rack analogue distortion emulation.

### Add instantaneous frequency and AM-FM decomposition

Add `plot_time_frequency_amfm.py` showing instantaneous frequency vs. time for a chirp alongside the STFT spectrogram for comparison. Add `docs/time_frequency/amfm.md` deriving the analytic signal, the Bedrosian theorem (conditions for valid AM-FM separation), and the phase unwrapping step. Add `notebooks/time_frequency_amfm.ipynb` demonstrating AM-FM decomposition on a chirp, a vibrato sine, and a broadband signal through the filterbank. AM-FM decomposition is the theoretical basis for instantaneous pitch detection, vibrato measurement, and formant tracking in natural sounds — applications that are otherwise obscured by the short-time Fourier frame boundary artefacts visible in the STFT.

### Add acoustic Doppler shift simulation

The Doppler effect shifts the apparent frequency of a moving sound source: when source and observer approach each other the received frequency is `f_r = f_s · (sound_speed + v_o) / (sound_speed − v_s)` where `v_s` and `v_o` are signed velocities along the source-observer axis. A positive `direction` means approaching (frequency rises), negative means receding (frequency falls). Add tests verifying: a 440 Hz sine with constant approach at 34 m/s (10% of 340 m/s sound speed) produces a received frequency of 484 Hz ± 2 Hz, at 0 velocity the output is unchanged within floating-point rounding, a linearly varying velocity produces a continuously sweeping pitch (instantaneous frequency monotonically increasing or decreasing), and array velocity inputs produce output identical to scalar inputs when both are constant. Add `plot_spatial_doppler.py` showing instantaneous frequency vs. time for a passing car (approach then recession), extracted via the AM-FM decomposition from `time_frequency/amfm.py`. Add `docs/spatial/doppler.py.md` deriving the sample-by-sample delay update from the classical Doppler formula. Add `notebooks/spatial_doppler.ipynb` demonstrating a flyby with time-varying velocity. Doppler shift is one of the few purely physical spatial effects that VCV Rack modules almost never implement; it is a useful building block for motion-simulated reverb tails and spatial sound design.

### Add Kuramoto coupled oscillator network

Add `docs/generators/kuramoto.md` deriving the critical coupling threshold and mean-field synchronization. Add `notebooks/generators_kuramoto.ipynb` demonstrating the disorder-to-synchronization transition as coupling increases. The Kuramoto model is the mathematical foundation of neural oscillations, firefly synchronization, and power-grid stability; as audio synthesis it uniquely produces multiple independent frequencies spontaneously locking into unison, a phenomenon achievable by no other oscillator topology in this codebase.

### Add free-reed physical model (harmonica / accordion reed)

Add `free_reed(freq, fs, duration, breath_pressure, reed_mass, reed_stiffness, reed_damping, nl_stiffness, channel_length)` to `src/python/physical/free_reed.py`. A free reed (harmonica, accordion, melodica, pipe organ reed stop) is a metal blade clamped at one end in a rectangular slot; unlike the clarinet or brass lip reed, which terminate a resonating bore, the free reed is itself the oscillator — its natural frequency sets the pitch. Model the reed as a damped nonlinear spring-mass system: `M · y'' + D · y' + K · y · (1 + K_nl · y²) = F_aero(y, p_breath)`, where `M = reed_mass`, `D = reed_damping`, `K = (2π · freq)² · M`, and `K_nl = nl_stiffness` adds cubic geometric nonlinearity that causes pitch bending at high breath pressure. The aerodynamic driving force uses a Bernoulli slot-flow model: for gap `h = h_slot − y` (where `h_slot = 0.5 mm` is the closed-slot height), `F_aero = sign(p_breath) · |p_breath| · A · (h_slot − y)² / h_slot²`; positive feedback when breath pressure exceeds local aerodynamic restoring force sustains oscillation above a threshold `p_breath`. The reed is hard-clamped for `y < −h_slot`. Discretise the ODE with the trapezoidal rule. When `channel_length > 0` (metres), a resonating air column of that length is added as a `waveguide_string` delay line with an open-end reflection at the far wall; the waveguide's scattering junction with the reed injects a pressure source equal to the volumetric flow `Q = A · y' · sign(p_breath)`, reinforcing specific harmonics as in a pipe organ reed stop. Return a `(t, output)` tuple of pressure at the reed exit plane. Add tests verifying: at `breath_pressure = 0` the output RMS is below −60 dB; at `breath_pressure = 0.5` (above the flutter threshold) the output RMS exceeds −20 dB within 0.1 s; the dominant FFT peak is within 5 Hz of `freq`; at `nl_stiffness = 0` doubling `breath_pressure` does not shift the peak by more than 5 Hz; at `nl_stiffness = 0.5` doubling `breath_pressure` shifts the peak upward by at least 3 Hz; `channel_length > 0` produces spectral peaks absent in the `channel_length = 0` case. ### Add Xenakis stochastic cloud synthesis (Poisson voice scheduler)

Add `stochastic_cloud(fs, duration, event_rate, pitch_dist, pitch_params, voice_model, voice_params, seed)` to `src/python/generators/` as a new `cloud.py` submodule. Xenakis defined "sound clouds" (*Formalized Music*, 1971) as textures whose individual note events are too dense to perceive individually — characterised by density, pitch range, and grain duration rather than melody. Inter-event times are drawn from an exponential distribution with rate `event_rate` (events/s). Each event spawns an independent voice at a pitch drawn from `pitch_dist`: `"uniform"` selects pitch uniformly in `[pitch_params["f_low"], pitch_params["f_high"]]` Hz; `"gaussian"` draws from a log-normal with `μ = pitch_params["f_center"]`, `σ = pitch_params["spread_oct"]` in octaves; `"scale"` quantises to the nearest step of a custom list `pitch_params["freqs"]`. `voice_model` selects the grain synthesis model: `"sine"` (single sine), `"modal"` (a `modal_bank` call with inharmonic metallic partial ratios `[1, 2.76, 5.40, 8.93, 13.34]` scaled to the event frequency), or `"pluck"` (a `pluck` call from `physical/string.py`). Each voice is Hann-windowed to a duration drawn exponentially with mean `voice_params["duration_mean"]` seconds. All active voices are summed and normalised to prevent clipping. Return `(t, signal, event_times, event_freqs)` where `event_times` and `event_freqs` record scheduled events. Export from `generators/__init__.py`. Add tests verifying: event count in a 10-second run is within 30% of `event_rate × 10`; inter-arrival times pass a Kolmogorov-Smirnov exponential test at α = 0.05; with `pitch_dist = "uniform"` and `f_low == f_high` all events have the same pitch; `seed` produces bit-identical output; output length matches the generator convention. Add `plot_generators_cloud.py` showing: a 20-second spectrogram demonstrating stochastic pitch scatter; the inter-event time histogram with an exponential fit; and a piano-roll scatter plot of `event_times` vs. `event_freqs` on a log frequency axis. Add `docs/generators/cloud.md` explaining Xenakis's Poisson-process composition theory and mapping `event_rate` to his density notation; deriving the time-bandwidth product `voice_params["duration_mean"] × event_rate` as the control between clearly pitched clouds (low product) and coloured noise (high product). Add `notebooks/generators_cloud.ipynb` demonstrating three Xenakis textures: sparse (2 events/s, long grains — individual pitches audible), moderate (20 events/s — texture fuses into bands), and dense (200 events/s — perceived as coloured noise). The Poisson voice scheduler is the only synthesis engine in this codebase where texture emerges from statistical density rather than a deterministic note sequence; it maps to a VCV Rack cloud-generator module accepting density, pitch range, and grain model as CV inputs — impossible to implement with a fixed number of physical oscillators.

### Add singing bowl physical model (Tibetan / crystal bowl)

Add `singing_bowl(freq, fs, duration, excitation_mode, strike_velocity, rub_force, rub_speed, damping, num_modes)` to `src/python/physical/singing_bowl.py`. A singing bowl (Tibetan singing bowl, crystal bowl, handpan) is a thick-walled hemispherical shell that can be struck or driven by rubbing a mallet around the rim. Its inharmonic mode frequencies follow the thick-shell approximation `f_m ≈ freq · m²(m²−1) / sqrt(m²+1) · C` for `m = 2, 3, …, num_modes+1` (m=0 and m=1 are non-radiating rigid-body modes), with `C` chosen so `f_2 = freq`. Each mode is a second-order resonator biquad (reusing `physical/resonator.py` infrastructure) with decay time `damping / f_m^0.3` (higher modes decay faster but less steeply than a bar). Each mode `m` exists as a degenerate pair: a cosine family `cos(m·θ)` and a sine family `sin(m·θ)`, both present simultaneously and differing only in azimuthal orientation. For `excitation_mode = "strike"`: project a half-sine mallet impulse of peak force `strike_velocity × mass` onto each mode via the mode shape at the strike point (`θ_strike = π/4`). For `excitation_mode = "rub"`: at each sample the mallet position is `θ_m(t) = 2π · rub_speed · t`; the stick-slip friction force `F[n] = rub_force · tanh(k · (v_stick − v_bowl))` (same `tanh` model as the bowed string) is projected onto both degenerate families via `cos(m·θ_m)` and `sin(m·θ_m)`; the transverse bowl velocity at the contact point is recomputed from the summed modal velocities each sample. Because both degenerate families are driven simultaneously by the rotating mallet, they lock to a 90°-phase-offset standing wave that rotates with the mallet, producing the characteristic spatial "wah-wah" beating audible at the output pickup (`θ_pickup = 0`). Return a `(t, output)` tuple. Add tests verifying: for `"strike"` the output decays below −40 dB within `duration`; the dominant FFT peak is within 5 Hz of `freq`; the second partial is between 2.7× and 3.0× `freq` (shell mode ratio); for `"rub"` the output RMS exceeds −20 dB in steady state (self-sustaining); `rub_force = 0` produces silence within 0.5 s; the AM envelope of the rub output shows a beating frequency within 10% of `2 · rub_speed` Hz (degenerate pair beating at twice the mallet rotation rate). Add `plot_physical_singing_bowl.py` showing: the strike impulse response with labelled modal partials; a steady-state rub spectrogram; and polar mode-shape plots for m = 2, 3, 4 visualising the degenerate cosine and sine families. Add `docs/physical/singing_bowl.md` deriving the thick-shell mode frequency formula, azimuthal degeneracy and its splitting, and the stick-slip coupling on a 2D surface, with a comparison to the bowed string model. Add `notebooks/physical_singing_bowl.ipynb` demonstrating strike vs. rub excitation with listening comparisons, varying `rub_speed` to change the beating rate, and a mode-identification panel. The singing bowl is the only instrument in this codebase that combines self-sustaining friction-driven oscillation with a 2D rotationally symmetric geometry; the degenerate mode pair and azimuthal beating are physically distinct from anything in `physical/bowed.py` or `physical/string.py`, targeting a VCV Rack meditation-bell module that outputs separate left/right pickups at different azimuthal positions for a rotating spatial effect.
